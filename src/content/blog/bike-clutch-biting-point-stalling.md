Your bike stops when starting because the clutch is coming out faster than the engine can take the load. The fix is learning your bike's **clutch biting point**: the small part of the lever's travel where the engine starts to pull the rear wheel.

Every beginner stalls: in an empty lane, at a signal with an auto honking behind, and on the day you think you've got it. What helps is knowing exactly what your left hand is doing wrong.

## What the clutch biting point actually is

When you pull the clutch lever in fully, the engine and the rear wheel are disconnected. Let it out fully, and they're locked together. In between there is a narrow zone, maybe a finger's width of lever travel, where they start to connect. That's the biting point (some people call it the friction zone).

Inside that zone:

- the engine note drops a little,
- the bike starts to creep forward or pull against the brake,
- you feel a light tug through the handlebar as the bike takes the load.

If you dump the lever through that zone in half a second, the engine gets the full weight of you and the bike all at once. At low revs it can't cope. It coughs and dies.

## The drill: find it before you need it

Do this on a flat, quiet stretch, not on a slope and not on your street with people watching. Engine on, bike in neutral to start.

**Step 1. Clutch in, first gear, no throttle.**
Pull the lever fully in, press the gear down into first. Keep your right hand off the throttle completely. Both feet on the ground.

**Step 2. Let the lever out slowly until the bike just wants to move.**
Count it out like a slow tap: a little, pause, a little more, pause. Listen. The moment the engine sound dips and the bike leans forward, stop. Hold the lever exactly there. That's your biting point.

**Step 3. Pull back in, and repeat ten times.**
Find it, hold it for two seconds, pull in. Your hand starts to remember where it is. On most bikes it sits around the middle of the lever's travel, but every bike is different, and a worn clutch cable moves it.

**Step 4. Creep without throttle.**
Find the biting point, hold it, and let the bike roll forward at walking pace with no throttle at all. Pull in, stop with the rear brake, and do it again. When you can creep five metres smoothly, add a whisker of throttle as you release the rest of the lever.

## Common mistakes and the fix

**Mistake: letting go of the lever like a switch.**
Fix: slow down through the middle of the lever. You can let it out quickly up to the biting point and after it, but not through it.

**Mistake: twisting the throttle hard to "help".**
Fix: a big handful of throttle with a clumsy clutch makes the bike jump, not stall. That's scarier. A small, steady throttle is enough on a flat road.

**Mistake: looking at the clutch lever or the front wheel.**
Fix: eyes up, looking where you want to go. Your hand can find the lever without your eyes.

**Mistake: starting in second or third gear.**
Fix: check the gear before you release. If you came to a stop without changing down, you're probably still in third. Pull the clutch in and tap down to first.

**Mistake: holding the front brake while releasing.**
Fix: on a flat road, use the rear brake to hold the bike if you need to. Grabbing the front brake while the clutch bites makes the bike nose-dive and wobble.

## What a trainer watches for

Standing next to a learner, we're mostly not watching the bike. We watch:

- **The left hand.** Does it pause at the biting point or snap open? A lot of stalling comes from a hand that is tense and opens all at once.
- **The right wrist.** Is it already twisting before the clutch bites? That usually means the learner is scared of stalling and over-revving to make up for it.
- **The feet.** Are both feet down until the bike is actually rolling? Lifting the feet too early makes people grab at the controls.
- **The engine sound.** A trainer can hear the dip before a stall and say "hold" a split second before it happens. That instant feedback is hard to get from a video.

## Starting on a slope or at a signal

Think of the ramp out of a building's garage. Hold the bike with the rear brake, find the biting point, feel the bike pull against the brake, add a little throttle, then ease off the brake as you let more clutch out. Practise this only after flat-ground starts feel easy.

At a busy signal with a bus behind you, don't rush. A smooth start a second later beats a stall in the middle of the junction. Our [road riding checklist](/blog/road-riding-checklist) covers more of what to do when traffic pressure kicks in.

## Questions people ask

**Is stalling bad for the bike?**
An occasional stall while learning won't harm a healthy bike. Keep the clutch in, shift to neutral if needed, restart and carry on.

**I can ride a scooty. Will a bike be easier for me?**
Your balance and road sense carry over, which helps a lot. The clutch is the new part. One of our learners started on a scooty on day 4 and was riding a geared bike by day 15.

**How long does it take to stop stalling?**
For most people, a few classes of focused practice. It comes back for a while when you're nervous, and that's fine too.
